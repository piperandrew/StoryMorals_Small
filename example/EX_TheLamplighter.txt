The Lamplighter of Vesper Lane

In the narrow town of Vesper Lane, where the fog arrived before the evening and
stayed long after, there lived an old lamplighter named Oren. Every dusk he
climbed the cobbled hill with his long brass pole and woke the gas lamps one by
one, and every dawn he climbed it again to put them to sleep. The townsfolk
rarely thanked him. They only noticed the lamps when one went dark.

Oren had a granddaughter, Mira, who thought the work beneath him. "The town
council could buy electric lights," she said, "and you could rest." But Oren
only smiled and handed her the pole. "A light is not only for seeing," he told
her. "It is a promise that someone is still awake, still watching, when the
night feels endless."

One winter a sickness moved through Vesper Lane, and the council, frightened and
thrifty, ordered the lamps left unlit to save oil. The streets went black. People
shut their doors and their hearts. But Mira remembered her grandfather's words.
Each night she took the brass pole and lit the lamps herself, paying for the oil
with the coins she had been saving for a journey she would now never take.

The small lights did not cure anyone. Yet the doctor, finding his way by them,
reached homes he would have missed in the dark. Neighbors, seeing a single lamp
burning, left bread on the steps of the sick. By spring the sickness had passed,
and the council took credit for the town's survival. Mira said nothing. She only
climbed the hill at dusk, as her grandfather had, and woke the lamps one by one.

When at last the electric lights came and the gas lamps were retired, the town
forgot Oren and Mira entirely. But the children of Vesper Lane grew up believing,
without quite knowing why, that it was a kind thing to leave a light on for a
stranger. And so the promise outlived the lamps.
